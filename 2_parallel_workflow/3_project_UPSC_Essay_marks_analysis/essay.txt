"""# Technology: A Useful Servant, but a Dangerous Master

When the first humans discovered fire, they gained the power to cook food, keep themselves warm and protect themselves from wild animals. Yet the same fire, when uncontrolled, could destroy entire settlements. Thousands of years later, humanity finds itself in a similar situation. The fire has changed its form—from stone tools and steam engines to artificial intelligence, biotechnology, nuclear energy and social media—but the fundamental question remains the same: **Do we control our tools, or do our tools begin to control us?**

Technology is neither inherently good nor inherently evil. It is an amplifier of human intention. When guided by wisdom, ethics and public purpose, it becomes one of humanity's greatest servants. When allowed to grow without accountability, it can become a dangerous master.

The history of civilisation is, in many ways, the history of technological progress. The invention of the wheel transformed transportation. The printing press democratised knowledge. The steam engine accelerated industrialisation. Electricity extended productive life beyond daylight. Vaccines defeated diseases that had once killed millions. The internet connected distant corners of the world within seconds.

India's own development journey demonstrates the transformative power of technology. Digital platforms have made banking, payments and government services more accessible. Direct Benefit Transfer has enabled welfare benefits to reach citizens more efficiently. Digital payment systems have allowed even small vendors to participate in the formal economy. Satellite technology supports weather forecasting, agriculture, disaster management and communication. Telemedicine can connect doctors in cities with patients in remote villages.

Thus, technology can act as a great equaliser.

A student in a small town with an internet connection can today access lectures from some of the world's finest universities. A farmer can obtain information about weather, crop prices and agricultural practices through a mobile phone. A small entrepreneur can sell products across the country without owning a large physical store.

However, every technological revolution also creates new forms of inequality.

The digital divide illustrates this contradiction. While technology opens enormous opportunities, those without internet access, digital literacy or suitable devices may find themselves excluded from education, employment and essential services. During the COVID-19 pandemic, online education helped millions of students continue learning, but it also exposed disparities between children who possessed laptops and stable internet connections and those who did not.

Therefore, the mere existence of technology does not guarantee equality. Access, affordability and capability determine whether technology becomes an instrument of empowerment or exclusion.

The relationship between technology and employment presents another complex challenge.

Automation has always displaced certain forms of work while creating others. The Industrial Revolution reduced dependence on manual labour in many sectors but generated entirely new industries and occupations. Today, artificial intelligence, robotics and machine learning are transforming sectors ranging from manufacturing and finance to healthcare and education.

Routine tasks can increasingly be performed by machines with greater speed and accuracy. This can increase productivity and reduce costs. Yet workers whose skills become obsolete may face economic insecurity.

The appropriate response cannot be to resist technological progress. History shows that societies cannot permanently halt innovation. Instead, governments, educational institutions and industries must prepare citizens for technological transitions through reskilling, lifelong learning and social protection.

Education itself must evolve. A system that merely trains students to memorise information risks becoming outdated in a world where machines can retrieve information instantly. Future education must emphasise critical thinking, creativity, ethical reasoning, communication and problem-solving—the qualities through which human beings complement rather than merely compete with machines.

Technology also poses profound challenges to democracy.

Social media has expanded the public sphere by giving ordinary citizens a platform to express opinions, organise movements and hold institutions accountable. Information that once travelled through a limited number of media organisations can now reach millions within minutes.

Yet the same platforms can spread misinformation, hate speech and manipulated content at extraordinary speed.

Algorithms often reward content that maximises engagement rather than truth. Sensational claims may travel faster than careful analysis. Deepfakes and AI-generated content further complicate the ability of citizens to distinguish reality from fabrication.

Democracy depends not merely on access to information but on access to reliable information. Therefore, digital literacy may become as important to modern citizenship as conventional literacy was in earlier generations.

Technology's influence is perhaps most visible in our personal lives.

Smartphones have made communication instantaneous, navigation effortless and knowledge easily accessible. Yet many people now experience an unusual paradox: they are more connected digitally and sometimes more isolated socially.

Notifications constantly compete for attention. Social media encourages comparison with carefully curated versions of other people's lives. Children may spend more time interacting with screens than with nature, families or communities.

The danger is not the device itself but the loss of conscious choice.

A smartphone should serve the individual. When an individual becomes unable to eat, work, study or rest without compulsively checking the screen, the relationship between master and servant begins to reverse.

Mahatma Gandhi's idea that there is more to life than increasing its speed remains remarkably relevant in the digital age. Efficiency is valuable, but efficiency cannot become the sole measure of human progress.

The same principle applies to artificial intelligence.

AI has extraordinary potential. It can assist doctors in detecting diseases, help scientists analyse complex data, support personalised education, improve disaster prediction and make public administration more efficient.

However, AI systems also raise questions of bias, accountability, privacy and human autonomy.

If an algorithm decides who receives a loan, who is shortlisted for a job or who becomes the subject of surveillance, society must ask: Who designed the algorithm? What data was used? Can its decision be challenged? Who is responsible when it causes harm?

Technology cannot become a shield behind which human responsibility disappears.

Similarly, biotechnology creates possibilities that previous generations could scarcely imagine. Gene-editing technologies may help cure hereditary diseases and improve agricultural productivity. But the ability to modify genetic characteristics also raises ethical concerns regarding designer babies, biological inequality and unintended consequences.

Nuclear technology presents perhaps the most dramatic illustration of the dual character of scientific progress. Nuclear energy can generate vast amounts of electricity with relatively low carbon emissions. Nuclear weapons, however, possess the capacity to destroy civilisation itself.

The same scientific knowledge can illuminate cities or annihilate them.

This duality demonstrates an important principle: **Scientific capability does not automatically produce moral wisdom.**

Human beings often ask, "Can this be done?" A mature civilisation must also ask, "Should this be done?", "Under what conditions?" and "Who bears the consequences?"

Environmental challenges further remind us that technological progress cannot be separated from ecological responsibility.

Industrialisation improved living standards across the world but also contributed to pollution, resource depletion and climate change. Technology helped create some of these problems, yet technology will also be essential in solving them.

Renewable energy, electric transportation, precision agriculture, efficient batteries, green hydrogen and carbon-capture technologies may contribute to a more sustainable economy.

However, technological solutions alone are insufficient. Endless consumption cannot become sustainable merely because products become slightly more efficient. Environmental responsibility also requires changes in behaviour, production models and ideas of prosperity.

Thus, technology must operate within the limits of ecology.

The challenge before governments is therefore not to choose between technology and tradition or between innovation and regulation. The challenge is to create **responsible innovation**.

Excessive regulation can discourage creativity and entrepreneurship. Absence of regulation can expose citizens to exploitation and harm. Good governance must find a balance.

A few principles can guide this approach.

First, technology should remain human-centred. Innovation must ultimately improve human dignity and quality of life.

Second, ethical safeguards must grow alongside technological capability. Privacy, transparency, fairness and accountability cannot be afterthoughts.

Third, access to technology must become more inclusive. Digital infrastructure, affordable connectivity and technological literacy should reach rural communities and disadvantaged groups.

Fourth, institutions must remain stronger than platforms. Democratic laws and constitutional values cannot be outsourced to private algorithms.

Finally, individuals must develop technological self-discipline. Governments can regulate corporations, but citizens must also learn when to switch off their screens, question information and protect their attention.

The future will not be a struggle between humans and machines. It will be a struggle over the values according to which humans use machines.

Technology has already become inseparable from modern civilisation. Rejecting it is neither desirable nor possible. What humanity requires is not technological pessimism but technological wisdom.

The ideal society is not one in which technology disappears. It is one in which technology becomes almost invisible—quietly improving healthcare, education, governance and productivity while leaving human beings free to pursue relationships, creativity, compassion and meaning.

Fire became civilisation's servant when humanity learned to contain it.

The same lesson applies today.

Artificial intelligence, biotechnology, nuclear science and digital networks possess extraordinary power. Whether that power creates a more humane civilisation or a more controlled, unequal and fragmented one will depend not on machines themselves but on the choices human beings make.

Ultimately, technology should expand human freedom, not replace human judgment; strengthen relationships, not weaken them; support democracy, not manipulate it; and increase prosperity without destroying the planet.

The most advanced society, therefore, will not be the one with the most powerful machines.

It will be the one wise enough to remain their master.
